3.1 Why this chapter matters

A currency crisis is what happens when markets stop believing a government’s exchange-rate promise. Reserves drain, interest rates spike, and the peg breaks — usually suddenly, after months in which officials insisted it would hold. A common working definition, from Frankel and Rose (1996), is a depreciation of at least 25 percent in a year that is also at least 10 percentage points faster than the year before.

Key questions

  • Why do pegs that look sound on Friday collapse by Monday?
  • Which crises were caused by bad policy, and which were self-fulfilling?
  • Why do currency crises so often arrive together with banking and debt crises?
  • What happens to a firm that earns in the collapsing currency and owes in dollars?

3.3 Three generations of explanation

Generation The story Classic case
First (Krugman, 1979) A government finances deficits that are incompatible with its peg; speculators attack when reserves are predictably about to run out. Latin America, 1970s–80s
Second (Obstfeld, 1994) The peg is sustainable, but defending it is costly; if enough traders bet against it, the government chooses to give up. The crisis is self-fulfilling. ERM, 1992
Third (Krugman, 1999; Chang and Velasco, 2001) Banks and firms borrowed in dollars; depreciation wrecks their balance sheets, so the currency and banking crises feed each other. Asia, 1997

NoteThe Ascent of Money · Niall Ferguson (book, 2008; PBS series)

Watch or read: Chapter 3, “Blowing Bubbles”, tells the story of John Law, the Scottish financier who ran France’s finances, flooded the country with paper notes to inflate his Mississippi Company, and watched both the shares and the notes collapse in 1720. It is the earliest crisis on Exhibit 3.1 and has the same plot as the rest: a currency promise the issuer could not keep.

What it gets wrong: historians dispute Ferguson’s claim that Law’s collapse set France on the road to revolution. The case for a direct link is weaker than the book suggests.
